An agent asserted a constraint stronger than the truth. It happened to yield the correct decision for the case at hand, so nothing challenged it, and it was one message away from being written into a durable record as settled fact with a second agent's endorsement attached. Premises that produce *wrong* answers get caught by the wrong answer. Premises that produce right answers have to be checked on their own, deliberately, and almost never are. **A confirmed conclusion is not evidence for the reasoning that reached it.**

**How to apply:**
- **Before a decision is recorded, list its premises separately from its outcome** and check each one at its source. "The brief told me X" and "X" sound alike and commit you to very different things.
- **A claim inherited from a brief, card or handoff is a claim, and blockers are its most perishable kind** — blockers are what get fixed. In one case the card cited as the blocker had already been rewritten to say "nothing is blocked today"; the quote stayed in circulation after the document retracted it.
- **A warning comment that is wrong is worse than none**, because it recruits the next reader into the bug with the authority of someone who seemed to have thought about it. A comment stating a guarantee ("these cannot disagree", "this is always safe") should name the mechanism that enforces it, so a reader can check the mechanism instead of trusting the assurance.
- **Record "this can be done and should not be" rather than "this cannot be done".** Both close a decision identically, but only the first lets a later reader reopen it on new information, and only the first is true when an option was found and rejected. A record that overstates impossibility is a premise failure aimed at the future.
